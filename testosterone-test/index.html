Testosterone Test: How It Works, Normal Levels and What Your Results Mean

Last updated: November 25, 2026 — For information only; not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment.

Quick answer: A testosterone test is a blood test that measures the amount of testosterone in your body. Blood is usually drawn from a vein in your arm, ideally between 7:00 and 10:00 in the morning, when levels peak. Fasting is usually not required unless other tests (glucose, cholesterol) run on the same sample. A sample adult male range for total testosterone is about 270–1080 ng/dL (≈ 9.4–37.4 nmol/L) — but interpretation always depends on your lab's own range, your age and your clinician.

What is a testosterone test?

A testosterone test measures the level of testosterone — the primary male sex hormone — in a blood sample. Testosterone drives sexual function and sperm production, and it also affects muscle and bone mass, energy, mood and red blood cell production. Women also produce testosterone, in much smaller amounts, in the ovaries and adrenal glands.

While circulating in your blood, part of the hormone binds to carrier proteins such as SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin) and albumin. That is why lab reports distinguish between total testosterone (bound plus free) and free testosterone (the unbound fraction that can enter cells). Depending on the situation, clinicians also order SHBG, LH, FSH, estradiol or prolactin alongside it.

When and why is a testosterone test done?

Common reasons a clinician orders the test include:

  • In adult men: persistent fatigue, low libido, fewer morning erections, erectile difficulties;
  • Loss of muscle mass and strength, unexplained weight gain;
  • Low mood, poor concentration, reduced motivation;
  • In women: irregular periods, increased body hair (hirsutism), resistant acne or a deeper voice being investigated;
  • Delayed or early puberty evaluation in adolescents;
  • Fertility workups that examine the hormone axis;
  • Monitoring during prescribed hormone therapy.

Because testosterone naturally peaks in the morning and falls during the day, a single borderline reading is usually repeated on another morning before any conclusion is drawn.

How the testosterone test works, step by step

  1. Clinical evaluation: See a clinician (primary care, internal medicine, urology or endocrinology) who decides whether testing is appropriate and which panel to order.
  2. Timing: Book the blood draw in the morning, ideally 7:00–10:00. Afternoon samples read lower and can be misleading.
  3. The blood draw: A phlebotomist cleans the skin, usually at the inside of your elbow, draws a small tube of blood with a fine needle, and removes it. The whole thing takes less than five minutes; you may feel a brief sting.
  4. Lab analysis and results: The sample is analyzed, typically in one to three business days. Your clinician interprets the value against your symptoms, your age and the other hormones in the panel — and repeats the test if needed.

Small practical tips: drink water before the draw (unless asked to fast), avoid hard exercise right before the test, and bring a list of any medications or supplements you use — several can affect the result.

Preparation: do you have to fast, and when should you test?

When only testosterone is measured, fasting is usually not required. Two exceptions are worth knowing:

  • If the same draw also includes fasting glucose, insulin or a lipid panel, the lab will usually ask for an 8–12 hour fast. Water is fine; skip food and caloric drinks.
  • Several medications can shift results — notably anabolic steroids, corticosteroids, some antidepressants and opioids. Tell your clinician everything you take; never stop a prescription medication on your own.
Your own clinician's and laboratory's preparation instructions always take precedence over general guidance like this guide.

Total testosterone vs free testosterone vs SHBG

The three numbers tell different stories and are read together:

  • Total testosterone: All testosterone in the sample, bound and free. It is the standard first-line screening test — inexpensive and well standardized.
  • Free testosterone: The fraction that enters cells. It can correlate better with symptoms and is often ordered when total testosterone sits in a borderline zone.
  • SHBG: The carrier protein that binds most testosterone. High SHBG (age, weight loss, some drugs and conditions) can make total testosterone look normal while the active fraction is low; low SHBG does the opposite.

To find the source of a low value, clinicians often add LH and FSH (pituitary vs testicular origin), plus estradiol and sometimes prolactin.

What are the symptoms of low testosterone?

In adult men, low testosterone (hypogonadism) typically shows up as:

  • Persistent fatigue and low motivation;
  • Reduced libido and fewer morning erections;
  • Loss of muscle mass and strength despite training;
  • Weight gain, especially around the waist;
  • Brain fog, low mood, irritability;
  • Poor sleep quality;
  • Longer term, reduced bone density.

Every one of these can have other causes — sleep apnea, thyroid disease, iron or vitamin D deficiency, depression, chronic stress, excess weight. That is why "feeling low" is not a diagnosis: it takes a morning blood test, often repeated, plus clinical evaluation. What are the symptoms of high testosterone?

High testosterone is usually caused by exogenous hormone or anabolic steroid use; natural overproduction in adult men is rare. In women it produces clearer symptoms:

  • Women: increased face and body hair, resistant acne, male-pattern hair loss, irregular periods, a deeper voice;
  • Men: increased aggression or impulsivity, acne, disrupted sleep; with steroid use, suppressed natural production and fertility risk over time;
  • Lab findings: elevated hematocrit (thicker blood).

An unexpectedly high result can also come from supplements or measurement method — tell your clinician about everything you use.

How are testosterone results interpreted?

Three things are read together, not the number alone:

  1. Draw time: A morning sample shows the true daily peak; an afternoon value naturally reads lower.
  2. Repetition: A borderline or low first result is typically repeated weeks later on another morning; no decision is made from one measurement.
  3. Symptoms and supporting tests: Two people with the same value can feel completely different. LH/FSH, SHBG, estradiol, ferritin and vitamin D help separate the causes.

How much does a testosterone test cost?

Cost depends on the facility, your country and what is in the panel:

  • Insurance / national health systems: When ordered by a physician with a documented reason, the test is commonly covered; small copays may apply.
  • Private labs: Total testosterone is usually among the cheapest items in a hormone panel; wider panels adding free testosterone, SHBG, LH, FSH and estradiol cost more. Ask the lab for current pricing.
  • Home kits: Priced per kit and usually per included parameter; wide panels approach private-lab prices.

Can you test testosterone at home?

Several brands sell finger-prick blood or saliva kits: you sample at home, mail it to a lab and read results online. Before buying one:

  • Check which method it uses (clinical serum methods vs saliva — saliva readings are not interpreted the same way as blood);
  • Check whether the reference ranges distinguish sex and age;
  • Treat any abnormal kit result as a screening hint that must be confirmed by a clinical blood draw;
  • Follow the kit's timing instructions carefully — finger-prick sampling easily misses the morning peak.

Which doctor does a testosterone test?

  • Adult men: urology or endocrinology; primary care and internal medicine can screen first.
  • Women: gynecology or endocrinology.
  • Children and adolescents: pediatrics or pediatric endocrinology.
  • Where it's done: hospital and private laboratories; having a clinician order and interpret the test improves the quality of the result.

Testosterone test: frequently asked questions

How do I test my testosterone levels?

With a blood test ordered by a clinician and analyzed by a laboratory. The draw takes under five minutes, ideally in the morning; results usually arrive in one to three business days.

Do I need to fast for a testosterone test?

Usually not when only testosterone is measured — but the draw should happen between 7:00 and 10:00 in the morning. If glucose or cholesterol tests run on the same sample, the lab may require an 8–12 hour fast.

Why is the test done in the morning?

Testosterone peaks in the morning and declines through the day. Afternoon samples read artificially low, so clinicians schedule the draw early.

How long do results take?

Typically one to three business days; specialized methods such as calculated free testosterone can take longer at some labs.

What is a normal testosterone level?

A sample adult male range for total testosterone is about 270–1080 ng/dL (≈ 9.4–37.4 nmol/L). Ranges vary by age, sex, method and lab — always use your own report's range with your clinician.

What's the difference between total and free testosterone?

Total measures all the hormone in blood; free measures the unbound fraction that can act on cells. When SHBG is high, total can look normal while free is low — so clinicians read them together.

What are the symptoms of low testosterone?

Fatigue, low libido, fewer morning erections, muscle and strength loss, weight gain, low mood and poor concentration. They overlap with many other conditions, so testing and clinical evaluation are required.

How much does a testosterone test cost?

It varies by facility and panel; physician-ordered testing is often covered by insurance or national systems. Wide panels cost more than a single total testosterone measurement.

Can I use an at-home testosterone kit?

Yes, for convenience — finger-prick and saliva kits exist — but they are less standardized than clinical labs. Confirm any abnormal result with a blood draw and review it with a clinician.

When should men get tested?

When symptoms such as persistent fatigue, low libido, erectile difficulties or unexplained muscle loss persist, or during fertility workups and hormone therapy monitoring. The clinician decides based on history and symptoms.

What if my result comes back low?

Clinicians usually repeat the measurement on another morning and add LH/FSH to identify the source. Treatment decisions weigh symptoms, repeated measurements and overall health — never self-prescribe hormones.
